# Draft report section: Verification, emergence and artificial life

**Philosophical position.** Formal verification and empirical exploration serve different purposes in artificial-life research. Verification can make a model's rules and implementation more trustworthy, but the choice of what counts as an organism, reproduction, adaptation or an interesting ecology still requires explicit concepts and evidence. A useful attitude is methodological modesty: prove the properties one has actually formalized, measure the behaviors one has actually observed, and expose the gap between them. A verified simulator can faithfully implement a model that is scientifically uninformative; a compelling animation can arise from a faulty implementation.

**Formal results (elementary reasoning, conditional on the stated model).** A binary outer-totalistic rule has 18 Boolean entries and therefore 2^18 possible rule tables. Local invariants include synchronous updates, eight-neighbor counting, legal output states and the independence of birth and survival entries. On a fixed finite grid of N cells with C semantic states, a deterministic update is a function on at most C^N configurations. By the pigeonhole principle, among C^N+1 consecutive configurations some pair must coincide; determinism then forces eventual periodicity. Binary age colors do not enlarge the semantic state space because they do not affect its future occupancy. This proves eventual recurrence for the fixed finite model, not that a particular run will settle quickly or have a short period.

**Formal methods and their limits.** Exhaustive reachability and model checking can, in principle, decide whether a reachable state violates a specified invariant on a fixed finite deterministic grid: enumerate successors until states repeat, or explore the transition graph. Model checking is only as relevant as its specification and abstraction. A 32x32 binary grid already has 2^1024 possible configurations, so a finite bound is not the same as a tractable search. Symmetry reduction and abstractions can help, but require care not to discard the behavior under investigation. With ideal stochastic noise one instead studies a finite transition graph with probabilities; with a concrete seeded generator, the generator state must be included in any claim about exact future repetition. The implementation's short-window recurrence detector is not an exhaustive model checker.

**Scope of decidability claims.** Finite fixed-size reachability must be distinguished from questions quantified over unbounded grids, arbitrary encoded computations or all times. Universality can connect suitable long-term prediction questions to undecidable computational problems; it does not make every property of every cellular automaton undecidable. The precise reduction and domain matter. A useful research starting point is Stephen Wolfram's [Twenty Problems in the Theory of Cellular Automata](https://content.wolfram.com/sw-publications/2020/07/problems-theory-cellular-automata.pdf), which discusses universality and undecidability. Before making a specific theorem claim about Life, locate the primary proof and state its boundary conditions and quantifiers explicitly. No general undecidability result is inferred from these finite experiments.

**Empirical observations and implementation evidence.** This project tests the 18 independent local choices, known Conway behavior, invalid input, seeded replay, noise endpoints, refractory transitions and short-period detection. Passing these tests supplies evidence, not a machine-checked proof of the entire engine or browser. In the recorded three-seed sample, B3678/S045678 reached terminal recurrence periods 1, 4 and 2 after 200 steps. These are observations for those initial states on that torus. Given exact deterministic implementation, repeating complete semantic states licenses continued periodicity for those runs; it does not establish the behavior of all initial conditions. Likewise, the label "disordered" summarizes a change-rate heuristic, not a theorem about chaos.

**Mechanistic hypothesis about ecological complexity.** Self-reproducing entities can introduce new levels of interaction because offspring can preserve organization across generations. If heritable variation also changes reproductive success, selection can alter the distribution of types. Competition for space or other model-specific resources can make success depend on the presence and frequency of other types; organisms then modify the environment in which later reproduction takes place. Feedback may produce changing niches, exploitation, interference or mutual dependence. Sayama and Nehaniv's [Self-Reproduction and Evolution in Cellular Automata: 25 Years after Evoloops](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03961) reviews work showing that variation and natural selection can occur among self-reproducing structures in deterministic cellular automata. This supports the possibility of evolutionary dynamics in that class of models, not their existence in the present B/S and Generations experiments.

**Speculation and limits.** Heredity, variation and context-dependent selection could make interactions increasingly diverse, but reproduction alone does not guarantee sustained novelty or open-ended evolution. A fixed finite deterministic universe ultimately recurs, and an apparently rich ecology may instead converge, exhaust available space or depend on externally injected perturbations. Whether added states and refractory delays permit durable reproducing organizations here is an unanswered experimental question. The current study contains no verified self-reproducing species and makes no claim of open-ended evolution.

**Proposed empirical standard for reproduction.** Visual resemblance is insufficient: translation of one object, an oscillator revisiting its own shape, random noise assembling a similar cluster, and a source emitting debris are different processes. Identify candidate parent and offspring boundaries, trace the causal construction of a spatially separate offspring, and test whether that offspring itself reproduces under the same rules. Repeat across generations, compare inherited features and variation, and use interventions such as removing the putative parent or isolating the offspring. If the claim is evolutionary reproduction rather than simple copying, measure heritability and differential reproductive success as well. Ambiguous individuality or boundaries should be reported rather than hidden by a visually appealing label.
